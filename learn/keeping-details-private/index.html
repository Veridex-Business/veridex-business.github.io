Lesson 5: Keeping your details private

What you'll learn

How to keep your details safe by asking one simple question: "Do they really need it?"

Steps

1. Ask: "Do they really need it?"

Your details are things like your phone number, your address, your email and your date of birth. Before you give them, ask yourself: "Do they really need it?"

Real details usually only need to go to:

  • your bank
  • your doctor or the HSE
  • the government
  • anything you pay for or need delivered

Even then, only give them when you contacted them yourself, or you've checked it's really them. Scammers often pretend to be a bank, the HSE or the government.

2. Everyone else gets a polite "no thanks"

Free Wi-Fi (internet in a café or shop), competitions, newsletters, shops asking at the till and random forms don't need your real details. You can say "no thanks", or leave the box blank.

Made-up example of a free Wi-Fi sign-up page (written for this page, not real)

"Free Wi-Fi! Enter your name, mobile number, home address and date of birth to connect. cafe-wifi.example.com"

Do they really need all that for Wi-Fi? No. Leave it, or give as little as you can.

3. Use a spare email for sign-ups

A spare email address is a second email address that you use only for sign-ups. It's like a second letterbox by the gate, so the post you care about stays separate. A family member can help you set one up. You choose and type the password yourself. Keep using your main email for your bank and anything important.

Made-up example of a spare email address

pat.signups@example.com

4. Keep your phone number and address to yourself

Don't post your phone number or home address where anyone can see it, like on a public page online. The more places that have your number, the more scam calls you may get. So give it only when it's really needed.

5. Hang up on unexpected calls and ring back

If someone rings out of the blue and asks for details, it's fine to hang up. Then ring back on a number you know, like the one on the back of your bank card. On a landline, wait until you hear the dial tone first, or use a different phone.

A caller may already know your name or address. That doesn't prove they're real.

Your bank will never send you a text with a link, or ask for your PIN, password or a code.

Try it

Read these made-up requests. For each one, ask: "Do they really need it?"

  • A: Your pharmacy asks for your address to deliver your prescription.
  • B: A shop asks for your email at the till "for offers".
  • C: A competition asks for your date of birth and mobile number.
  • D: Your doctor's surgery asks for your phone number to book an appointment.

Answer: A and D really need it. For B and C, a polite "no thanks" is fine, or use your spare email.

Stop, Check, Ask

If someone calls, texts or asks for your details out of the blue:

  1. Stop. Don't give any details. It's fine to hang up.
  2. Check. Hang up. Then ring back on a number you know, like the one on the back of your bank card. On a landline, wait until you hear the dial tone first, or use a different phone.
  3. Ask yourself: "Do they really need it?" And ask someone you trust.

Your bank will never send you a text with a link, or ask for your PIN, password or a code. If you think you may have shared details, ring your bank straight away on the number on the back of your card. Then report it to your local Garda station. It's not your fault, and it's always fine to ask for help.

Keeping Your Details Private

  • Before you give details, ask: "Do they really need it?"
  • Real details go only to your bank, your doctor or the HSE, the government, and things you pay for, and only when you contacted them.
  • Everyone else gets a polite "no thanks", or a blank.
  • A spare email is a second email address just for sign-ups.
  • Don't post your phone number or address where anyone can see it.
  • Unexpected call? Hang up. Ring back on a number you know. On a landline, wait until you hear the dial tone first.
  • Your bank will never send you a text with a link, or ask for your PIN, password or a code.
  • Not sure? Ask someone you trust.
